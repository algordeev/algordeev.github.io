Autonomous Tram System

Context
Individual robotics project · WRO 2021
My focus
Embedded control, custom PCBs & system integration
4Arduino Nano controllers: tram, remote, switch and signals
3Custom PCBs: tram, remote and traffic lights
2 + 22 NRF24L01 wireless modules and 2 RFID readers

The challenge

Build a physical model tram that can control its movement, detect obstacles and communicate with infrastructure through an integrated embedded system.

My work

I developed the model’s embedded control system, combining Arduino Nano hardware, C++ firmware and custom PCBs. The firmware handles motion control, obstacle detection, manual override and RFID-based infrastructure interaction; the electronics integrate motor drivers, wireless modules, distance sensors and actuators.

  • Replaced breadboard wiring with custom EasyEDA PCBs to make the electronics more reliable.
  • Programmed motor control, obstacle stopping, the station sequence and remote speed/direction commands.
  • Built RFID-triggered control for the track-switch servo and two-direction traffic lights.
  • Adapted the tram body for electronics and designed custom infrastructure parts and the remote enclosure.

Four controllers, two ways to coordinate

The remote and tram share an NRF24L01 radio link. The switch and signals work independently, reading RFID tags on passing vehicles. A reed switch triggers the tram’s programmed station stop.

Remote

Joystick and buttons → NRF24L01 speed, direction, mode and stop commands.

Tram

VL53L0X and reed switch → Arduino Nano → TB6612FNG driver and geared motor.

Track switch

RFID tag → MFRC522 reader → Nano → servo position for the configured route.

Traffic lights

RFID tag → MFRC522 reader → Nano → programmed signal-change sequence.

How the model handles a stop

  1. Obstacle detected

    In autonomous mode, a distance reading of 70 mm or less disables both motor-direction outputs.

  2. Station reached

    The reed switch starts a slow 2-second approach, followed by a 5-second stop.

  3. Manual control

    The remote selects speed, direction and mode; its stop command disables the motor outputs.

Energy-efficiency concepts

The project report explores regenerative braking with flywheel storage, solar-powered stops and piezoelectric paving for auxiliary power. These are proposed directions supported by calculations and demonstrations, rather than measured energy savings from the model tram.

Outcome

A working autonomous tram model that won the WRO 2021 Russian National Final and competed at the international final. The physical prototype later became the starting point for my autonomous tram digital twin.

Key features

Motion control · Obstacle detection · Manual override · RFID infrastructure

From model to electronics

Mechanical base: tram body, rails and track-switch designs by Depronized; modified for this prototype, with custom infrastructure and electronic integration.

  • Co-developed Command-based undo and added gameplay dialogs and player icons.
  • Managed the backlog, maintained the wiki and demonstrated features at sprint reviews.

Three ways to decide a purchase

The AI uses explicit purchasing rules. Each difficulty changes how an opponent decides whether to buy a property or railroad.

Easy

Buys when its cash covers the price.

Medium

Requires cash of at least 120% of the price to keep a buffer.

Hard

Requires cash above the price and a rent-to-price ratio greater than 5%.

Keeping the game state consistent

The Swing interface, controller and game model separate presentation from rules. JSON save/load preserves game state; a command history supports undo. In multiplayer, a TCP host coordinates client messages and turn handling.

  1. Swing interfaceBoard, dialogs and player information
  2. ControllerActions, undo history and save/load
  3. Game modelPlayers, tiles and AI strategies

From board rules to multiplayer

  3. Sprint 6 · Playing together

    Deeper undo, pre-game figure selection, tax dialogs and an alpha host/join multiplayer mode.

A playable Java desktop game with a themed board, AI opponents with three difficulty levels, save/load, undo and a networked multiplayer mode. The team delivered the game through six Scrum sprints, with source code and development documentation available on GitHub.

The challenge

Plan coordinated missions for several autonomous surface vehicles monitoring cyanobacterial blooms. Routes must balance collision avoidance, route overlap and workload across the fleet.

Simulated monitoring missions

Both runs use the median-hypervolume repeat among five repeats. The maps show recorded cyanobacteria motion and planned trajectories; synchronised charts show local projected density (model units), horizontal speed over ground (m/s) and probe depth (m). Density is a model projection, not calibrated concentration.

My work

During my ISCAR internship at Universidad Complutense de Madrid, I extended an existing single-vehicle MATLAB planner to multi-vehicle scenarios. I worked with NSGA-II and NSGA-III, ran repeated simulations for two- and three-vehicle fleets, and analysed Pareto fronts using hypervolume evaluation and non-parametric statistical comparisons.

  • Gave every vehicle its own path variables, with collision avoidance between vehicles
  • Counted collected density once across the fleet, so overlapping routes are not rewarded twice
  • Replaced total distance with the longest individual route as the distance objective
  • Migrated the optimiser from NSGA-II to NSGA-III
  • Automated repeated runs (five per fleet size, lake and configuration) and the analysis pipeline

A more realistic distance objective

Summing every route treats the fleet as if it shared one battery. Minimising the longest individual route matches reality: each vehicle carries its own battery.

Outcome

Multi-vehicle planning experiments with trajectory plots, comparative figures and an internship report. The work provided a basis for assessing trade-offs between competing mission objectives and comparing optimisation methods across repeated simulation runs.

Autonomous Tram Digital Twin

Context
Independent research prototype
My focus
Network operations, control & traction energy
3Network scenarios: Route 2, Routes 2 + 21 and Konak Tram
2Cities modelled: Nizhny Novgorod and Izmir
3Energy strategies compared under identical service conditions

The challenge

Extend a physical autonomous tram prototype into a simulation environment for studying how several trams interact with passengers, infrastructure and service schedules.

My work

I develop the tram simulation and control logic, including passenger generation, depot dispatch, terminal holding and headway regulation. The model includes vehicle dynamics, traffic signals and track switches, with scenarios based on Nizhny Novgorod and Izmir. I also work on energy analysis with regenerative braking and flywheel storage, using a C++ core and a TypeScript interface.

  • Built a C++/WebAssembly control core with a TypeScript scenario and experiment layer.
  • Integrated passenger queues, boarding, variable dwell, terminal slots and depot dispatch.
  • Modelled signals, turnout locks and shared-track conflicts alongside vehicle dynamics.
  • Added regenerative reuse, flywheel storage and headless comparisons with service and energy metrics.

Two cities, different operating problems

Route 2 models a city-centre ring. The combined Routes 2 + 21 scenario adds shared tracks and junction conflicts. Izmir models the Konak corridor with two-berth terminals and the Halkapınar depot.

Control decisions separate from the interface

A fixed 50 ms simulation step connects the scenario layer to the C++ authority. The interface displays snapshots and sends operator commands; the same engine also runs without a browser for repeatable experiments.

  1. C++ / WebAssemblyMotion, braking, doors, signals, turnouts and energy decisions
  2. TypeScript orchestrationNetwork data, passenger demand, service plans and experiments
  3. React + CanvasInteractive map, operator controls and live analysis panels

Passenger service meets vehicle dynamics

Time-dependent arrivals create queues at stops. Boarding and alighting affect dwell, while passenger mass changes vehicle dynamics. Terminal departure slots, headway checks and depot release regulate service; signals and turnout locks constrain movement through shared infrastructure.

Comparing three energy strategies

Comparisons hold the network, fleet, service clock and duration constant. Grid energy is normalized per vehicle-kilometre, with peak power and departure adherence reported alongside it.

No wayside storage

Reference case with direct regenerative reuse.

Reactive flywheels

Capture available braking energy and return it to traction demand.

Predictive storage

Forecast-based state-of-charge and peak-power dispatch.

Research status: energy results are comparative simulation estimates. Vehicle parameters and reconstructed traction sections still need calibration against real measurements.

Outcome

An interactive web simulator with a live demo, time-distance diagrams and energy statistics. It provides a platform for comparing operating strategies and exploring how delays, passenger demand and dispatch decisions affect the simulated network. Development and refinement of the models are ongoing.
